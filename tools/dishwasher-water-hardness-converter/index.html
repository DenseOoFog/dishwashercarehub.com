Where to find your water hardness

  • Search your local water utility’s annual water-quality report or hardness page.
  • For a private well or household softener, use a recent laboratory result or a hardness test made for water—not a pool-only interpretation chart.
  • Read the unit beside the result. A value of “10” means very different things in gpg, °dH, and mg/L.
  • If your result lists separate calcium and magnesium instead of total hardness as CaCO₃, do not enter either value alone.

Classification and conversion basis

The U.S. Geological Survey classifies 0–60 mg/L as soft, 61–120 mg/L as moderately hard, 121–180 mg/L as hard, and more than 180 mg/L as very hard, expressed as calcium carbonate. One U.S. grain per gallon is approximately 17.1 mg/L.

This calculator uses: 1 gpg = 17.12 mg/L; 1 °dH = 17.848 mg/L; 1 °fH = 10 mg/L; 1 °Clark = 14.254 mg/L; and 1 mmol/L as CaCO₃ = 100.0869 mg/L. Rounded display values may differ slightly from a manual’s conversion table.

Common questions

Dishwasher Water Hardness Converter FAQ

  • Why are water-hardness values reported in different units? Utilities, test strips, softeners, detergent labels, and appliance manuals may use ppm, mg/L as calcium carbonate, grains per gallon, or German, French, and English degrees. Converting the same measured value makes those documents easier to compare.
  • Where should I get the number to convert? Use a current local water-quality report, a suitable test result taken at the dishwasher supply, or information from the water-treatment provider. Do not copy a regional average when a softener, private well, building system, or seasonal source can change the actual water.
  • Will the hardness band tell me an exact salt or detergent setting? No. It supplies a converted measurement and a general classification. The dishwasher manual and product label determine whether the machine has a softener, which scale its setting uses, and the allowed salt, rinse-aid, and detergent adjustments.
